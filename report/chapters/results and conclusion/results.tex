\subsection{Model 1}
We ran the first model 4 times. Twice for absorption, where in one iteration we assumed a constant gas density, and in the other iteration we assumed a variable gas density. The results of these two iterations are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:model1_absorption_comparison_absorption}. The numerical solution is compared with the analytical solution for the constant gas density case, and the solid density and gas density are shown for the variable gas density case. We applied the same two iterations for desorption, and the results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:model1_absorption_comparison_desorption}. 

As we see the cases where the case where we have an analytical solution, the numerical solution matches the analytical solution very well. The cases where we have a variable gas density show that the gas density decreases over time, while the solid density increases over time. For absorption with a constant gas density, we see that the battery is fully saturated (has a filled fraction larger then $0.999$) after $xxx$ seconds, while for absorption with a variable gas density, we see that the battery is fully saturated after $xxx$ seconds. For desorption with a constant gas density, we see that the battery is fully desaturated (has a filled fraction smaller then $0.001$) after $xxx$ seconds, while for desorption with a variable gas density, we see that the battery is fully desaturated after $xxx$ seconds. 

The first results have been done with a porosity of $\epsilon=0.4$. We have also run the model with a porosity of $\epsilon=0.5$ and $\epsilon=0.6$. The results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:model1_epsilon_comparison}. We see that the higher the porosity, the faster the battery is saturated, but the lower the total solid density. This is because a higher porosity means that there is more space for the gas to occupy, which means that there is less space for the solid to occupy.

Lastly we made a plot of the time step size of the automatic solver for model 1. The results are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:model_1_variable_gas_desorption}. We see that the time step size increases as the model reaches a steady state where $|d\mathbf{u}/dt|$ becomes smaller and smaller. This is because the automatic solver adjusts the time step size based on the rate of change of the solution, and as the solution approaches a steady state, the rate of change decreases, allowing for larger time steps to be taken.

\subsection{Model 2}
For model 2 we first use a variable set with low P\'eclet number. This is less realistic, but it is a good test case to see if the model works.

Figure~\ref{fig:model2_peclet_comparison} compares snapshots of the gas and solid densities at $t=0.5s$ for two different P\'eclet numbers. In both cases, $v=1.0m/s$ and $h=0.01m$. In Figure~\ref{fig:model2_low_peclet}, the diffusion coefficient is $\mu=0.01m^2/s$, corresponding to $\mathrm{Pe}=1.0$. The gas-density profile remains smooth, while the solid density increases in the regions where the gas density is higher. Figure~\ref{fig:model2_high_peclet} shows the result for $\mu=10^{-5}$, corresponding to $\mathrm{Pe}=1000$, with the DAE time integration. At this high P\'eclet number, oscillations appear in the gas-density solution because SUPG stabilization has not yet been applied. Since the IMEX method uses the, in this model, convection term as fully implicit it does not seem to create oscillations.

Figure~\ref{fig:model2_saturation_comparison} shows the evolution of the solid saturation at different positions in the domain, together with the spatially averaged saturation. For the low-P\'eclet-number case with non-realistic parameter values, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:model2_low_pe_dae}, the saturation at the inlet follows the analytical solution from Model~1 for a constant gas density. This happens because the gas density at the inlet is our constant boundary condition, and it therefor acts as a Model~1 with constant gas density. The saturation at $x=L$ responds later, while the average saturation remains between the inlet and outlet values. With the realistic parameters and SUPG stabilization, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:model2_realistic_dae_supg}, the filling time increases from approximately $3s$ to approximately $200s$. The saturation curves at the different positions are then nearly identical. This is because, although the velocity is only $v=0.1m/s$, the gas fills the metre long domain in approximately $10s$, which is much shorter than the characteristic absorption time. Thus, the gas density becomes distributed throughout the domain before a substantial amount of gas is absorbed, and Model~1 provides a reasonable approximation for this case. Initially, this might seem like the gas velocity has little impact. However, it is important to note that temperature has not yet been included. Absorption generates heat, and the resulting temperature increase reduces the absorption rate. Because velocity transports the temperature with the gas, the velocity will play a big role if temperature is included..

\subsection{Model 4}

For validation of the numerical implementation, three discretizations of the same governing equations were constructed. Two models use the finite-element spatial discretization derived above, of which one is integrated using the IMEX scheme and the other using the IDA implementation with the analytically constructed Jacobian. In addition, an independent finite-difference implementation was developed for comparison.

At relatively low P\'eclet numbers, no significant difference is observed between the FEM solutions obtained with IMEX and IDA. This indicates that both time-integration methods correctly reproduce the solution when diffusion is sufficiently strong relative to convection. For increasingly convection-dominated cases, however, the IDA implementation was found to remain more robust, whereas the IMEX method becomes more restrictive.

Figure~\ref{fig:model_4_validation} compares the FEM-IMEX solution with the independently implemented finite-difference solution for a low-P\'eclet-number case. The two methods show close agreement. A small difference can be observed in the finite-difference solution, which is likely caused by the additional numerical diffusion introduced by the upwind discretization of the convective terms. This artificial diffusion smooths steep spatial gradients and therefore slightly modifies the solution even when both discretizations approximate the same governing equations.

\subsection{Model 5}
A snapshot of the results of the fifth model is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:model5_snapshot}. The simulation was performed using the IDA time-integration method with the KLU sparse linear solver and the implemented analytical Jacobian. Checkerboard-like patterns can be observed in the velocity magnitude $|\mathbf{v}|$ and in both velocity components $v_x$ and $v_y$. These oscillations indicate that the two-dimensional formulation still suffers from numerical instability in the velocity field. Nevertheless, the overall filling behaviour of the battery remains very similar to that observed for Model~1. The gas pressure and gas density redistribute through the battery much faster than hydrogen is transferred into the metal hydride. Consequently, the solid hydrogen density remains nearly spatially uniform during most of the filling process, and the total filling curve is therefore close to that of the spatially uniform Model~1.
